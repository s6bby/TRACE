Pattern of Behavior (describe the pattern of behavior(s) that impedes the student’s learning or the learning of others):
Elena demonstrates a persistent pattern of frustration behaviors during high physical demand tasks that impedes her participation and instructional access. Specifically: (1) Pushing SGD or adaptive materials away from the workspace, occurring 1–2 times per instructional period during extended fine motor or device-use tasks; (2) Loud vocalizations and turning head away from task when motor fatigue or frustration peaks, occurring 1–2 times per period; (3) Ceasing motor effort mid-task (stopping use of switch, eye-gaze, or adapted device) without signaling need for break, occurring 2–3 times per day; (4) Reluctance to re-engage following a brief rest, requiring 3–5 adult prompts to return to task. These behaviors occur specifically during physically effortful tasks requiring sustained fine motor or eye-gaze activation. They are absent during receptive tasks, SGD browsing, and preferred low-demand activities. Function is assessed as escape from physically effortful tasks that exceed fine motor endurance and communication of fatigue or discomfort.
Instructional and Environmental Conditions or Circumstances (describe the instructional and environmental conditions or circumstances that trigger/contribute to the pattern of behavior(s) being addressed):
Frustration behaviors are most frequent under the following conditions: (1) During extended fine motor tasks requiring continuous switch or eye-gaze activation for more than 10 consecutive minutes without a break; (2) During afternoon sessions (after 1:00 PM) when physical and cognitive fatigue is greatest; (3) When SGD response latency is high and communication partners do not wait for message completion; (4) When the physical demand of a task is not adjusted following visible signs of fatigue (tremor increase, reduced activation accuracy). Antecedent FBA data indicate that proactive breaks every 8–10 minutes of fine motor work reduce frustration behaviors by approximately 60%. Behaviors are absent during low-demand receptive instruction and preferred non-motor activities. Function is assessed as escape from tasks exceeding fine motor endurance and communication of physical discomfort or fatigue when SGD communication is too slow.
Alternative Behaviors and Skills to be Taught and Monitored (describe the alternative behaviors and skills to be taught and monitored in order to address the pattern of behaviors and the instructional and environmental conditions/ circumstances previously described):
The following alternative behaviors and skills will be explicitly taught and monitored: (1) Break Request via SGD: Elena will be directly taught and rehearsed to use her SGD to activate the “break” or “I need help” message to request a break from a physically demanding task, rather than pushing materials away or ceasing motor effort. Staff will acknowledge SGD break requests within 15 seconds and provide a 3–5 minute rest before prompting return to task. (2) Fatigue Signal: Elena will be taught to use a simple low-effort signal (e.g., activating a designated “tired” button on her SGD home page) to communicate motor fatigue before frustration peaks. Staff will conduct a proactive check-in after every 8–10 minutes of sustained fine motor work regardless of whether Elena signals. (3) Task Chunking and Visual Timer: All fine motor tasks will be divided into timed segments (8–10 minutes maximum) with a visual countdown timer visible to Elena. At the end of each segment, a 3-minute rest is built in. Elena will be taught to watch the timer and anticipate the rest, reducing the unpredictability that contributes to frustration. (4) Reduced Demand Pathway: If Elena produces a frustration behavior, staff will offer a brief choice: “Break for 3 minutes, then try again” or “Switch to [lower-demand activity] and come back to this.” This teaches Elena that communicating fatigue is effective, reinforcing SGD use over behavioral communication. (5) Self-Advocacy Practice: During SLP pull-out sessions, Elena will rehearse using her SGD to request accommodations (break, different task, help) with unfamiliar adults, building generalization of self-advocacy skills for post-secondary settings.
Reinforcers of Desired Behaviors (describe how the alternative behaviors will be reinforced):
Desired behaviors will be reinforced using the following strategies, selected based on Elena’s identified motivators (music, photography, peer interaction, and adult praise): (1) Behavior-Specific Praise: Staff will provide immediate, specific verbal and visual praise when Elena uses her SGD to request a break or signals fatigue proactively (e.g., “Elena, you used your break button — great communication! We will take a break right now”). Praise is delivered warmly and respects her communication pace (allow full SGD activation before responding). (2) Break as Direct Reinforcer: When Elena uses SGD to request a break, the break is provided immediately (within 15 seconds) without additional demands. This directly reinforces SGD communication over behavioral communication of fatigue. (3) Activity Choice Following Task Completion: Upon completing a fine motor task to criterion, Elena selects a preferred activity from a visual choice board (music, photography app, peer conversation, independent SGD browsing). This reinforces task completion and provides meaningful rest. (4) Competence-Building Recognition: OT and SLP will regularly share specific evidence of Elena’s SGD skill growth with Elena and her family, framing her communication development as preparation for the independence she has expressed desire for post-secondary. This addresses the demoralization that can accompany high-effort motor tasks.
Methods to Ensure Consistency of Implementation (describe the method(s) that will be used to ensure the consistency of the implementation of the positive behavioral interventions across the student’s school-sponsored instruction or activities (e.g., data collection, frequency, assessment of effectiveness of BIP, additional training for staff, etc.)):
The following methods will be used to ensure consistent implementation of the BIP across all school-sponsored instruction and activities: (1) Data Collection: The special education teacher or paraeducator will collect a daily frequency tally of SGD break requests, frustration behaviors (pushing materials, vocalizations, motor cessation), and return-to-task successes. Time of day distribution will be tracked to monitor fatigue patterns. Data will be reviewed weekly and graphed monthly for IEP team review. (2) Staff Communication and Training: All staff who work with Elena (special education teacher, paraeducators, SLP, OT, PT, general education teachers in inclusion settings) will receive a copy of the BIP and attend a 30-minute training that includes: how to respond to SGD break requests within 15 seconds, how to implement the 8–10 minute task chunking protocol, how to recognize early fatigue signals, and partner-augmented communication strategies for SGD interaction. The BIP will be reviewed monthly during IEP team check-ins. (3) Assessment of BIP Effectiveness: Frustration behavior frequency and SGD break request rate will be graphed monthly. The team will compare data to baseline to evaluate progress toward IEP Goal #4 (self-advocacy/independence). If frustration behaviors do not decrease within 6 weeks of consistent implementation, the team will convene to review and consider further task load reduction or consultation with the assistive technology specialist. (4) Parent Communication: Parents will receive a copy of the BIP and will be informed of Elena’s progress through quarterly written reports. Staff will contact the family if frustration behaviors increase in frequency or if SGD equipment issues arise. Parent input on Elena’s fatigue patterns and home device use will be solicited quarterly to maintain home-school consistency.
