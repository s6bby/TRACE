Pattern of Behavior (describe the pattern of behavior(s) that impedes the student’s learning or the learning of others):
Marcus demonstrates a persistent pattern of noncompliance and verbal refusal during non-preferred academic tasks that reduces his instructional access and learning. Specifically: (1) Verbal refusals (stating “I’m not doing that,” “This is boring,” or “I don’t get it”) occurring 2–3 times per academic class period; (2) Task avoidance behaviors (putting head down, crumpling or pushing away materials, making off-task comments) occurring 2–4 times per academic class period; (3) Requesting to leave the room (bathroom, water) to avoid task initiation, occurring 1–2 times per class; (4) Verbal arguing with staff when redirected back to tasks, occurring 1–2 times per week. These behaviors occur specifically during paper-pencil academic tasks and abstract, non-functional assignments. Behaviors are absent or minimal during cooking/life skills, music, community-based instruction, and preferred social activities. Function is assessed as escape from tasks perceived as too difficult or having no perceived functional relevance.
Instructional and Environmental Conditions or Circumstances (describe the instructional and environmental conditions or circumstances that trigger/contribute to the pattern of behavior(s) being addressed):
Noncompliance and refusal behaviors are most frequent under the following conditions: (1) During academic instruction periods requiring paper-pencil or abstract tasks without functional relevance (e.g., grade-level reading passages, multi-step math worksheets); (2) At the beginning of a task when the demand is presented without a warm-up or visual scaffold; (3) When tasks are perceived as too long or when no break is offered; (4) When a non-preferred task immediately follows a preferred activity with no transition warning. Antecedent FBA data indicate that approximately 80% of refusal behaviors occur within the first 3 minutes of a non-preferred task being assigned. Behaviors are absent during cooking/life skills, music, PE, and community-based instruction. Function of behaviors is assessed as escape from tasks perceived as non-functional, too difficult, or having no clear purpose.
Alternative Behaviors and Skills to be Taught and Monitored (describe the alternative behaviors and skills to be taught and monitored in order to address the pattern of behaviors and the instructional and environmental conditions/ circumstances previously described):
The following alternative behaviors and skills will be explicitly taught and monitored: (1) Task Request Procedure: Marcus will be directly taught and rehearsed the skill of using a verbal or written request card to ask for help, a break, or a modified version of a task, rather than refusing or arguing. Staff will respond to appropriately communicated requests within 30 seconds and honor them whenever possible. (2) “First-Then” Strategy: Marcus will be explicitly taught a “First-Then” routine (“First you do [non-preferred task], then you earn [preferred activity]”) posted visually at his workspace. This provides a clear and predictable structure that reduces the perceived open-endedness of non-preferred task demands. (3) Task Warm-Up Routine: Marcus will begin each academic period with a brief (2-minute), low-demand warm-up task in the same subject before transitioning to the primary assignment. This reduces the abruptness of non-preferred task demands and decreases refusal at task onset. (4) Choice Within Structure: Marcus will be offered structured choices within non-preferred tasks (e.g., “Do you want to do the first 3 problems now and the last 3 after your break, or all 6 now?”) to increase perceived autonomy and reduce noncompliance while maintaining academic expectations. (5) Self-Monitoring Card: Marcus will use a brief daily self-monitoring card to rate his task completion effort (1–3 scale) and identify one strategy he used. This will be reviewed with the special education teacher at the end of each academic period.
Reinforcers of Desired Behaviors (describe how the alternative behaviors will be reinforced):
Desired behaviors will be reinforced using the following strategies, selected based on Marcus’s identified motivators (cooking/food, cars/automotive content, peer recognition, and choice-based activities): (1) Behavior-Specific Praise: Staff will provide immediate, specific verbal praise when Marcus uses his request card, completes a task using First-Then, or begins a non-preferred task without refusing (e.g., “Marcus, you used your request card and got right back to it — that’s exactly what I want to see”). Praise will be delivered immediately and enthusiastically. (2) Token Economy: Marcus will earn tokens for each non-preferred task he initiates without refusal and each successful use of his request procedure. Upon earning 5 tokens, he may select a reward from a preference menu (e.g., 10 minutes of cooking activity, looking at automotive magazines, playing a preferred game with a peer, or a preferred classroom job). (3) Functional Relevance Framing: Staff will explicitly connect non-preferred academic tasks to Marcus’s vocational interests and post-secondary goals (e.g., “This reading practice will help you read job application forms”; “This money math is what you’ll use when you’re working”). This increases the perceived relevance of non-preferred tasks and reduces the escape motivation. (4) Preferred Task Access as Contingency: Following successful completion of a non-preferred academic task, Marcus will transition to a preferred activity (cooking, vocational task, peer activity) as a natural positive consequence, reinforcing task completion over refusal.
Methods to Ensure Consistency of Implementation (describe the method(s) that will be used to ensure the consistency of the implementation of the positive behavioral interventions across the student’s school-sponsored instruction or activities (e.g., data collection, frequency, assessment of effectiveness of BIP, additional training for staff, etc.)):
The following methods will be used to ensure consistent implementation of the BIP across all school-sponsored instruction and activities: (1) Data Collection: The special education teacher or paraeducator will collect daily frequency data on verbal refusals, task avoidance behaviors, and successful request card uses using a structured per-period tally sheet. Task initiation latency (time between task assignment and task onset) will be recorded on a weekly basis during direct observation. Data will be reviewed weekly and graphed monthly for IEP team review. (2) Staff Communication and Training: All instructional staff who work with Marcus (special education teacher, life skills teacher, paraeducator, community-based instruction coordinator) will receive a copy of the BIP and attend a 30-minute orientation prior to implementation. Training will include: delivery of First-Then structure, appropriate response to task request cards, consistent use of behavior-specific praise, and how to frame academic tasks in terms of vocational relevance. The BIP will be reviewed monthly during IEP team check-ins to assess fidelity. (3) Assessment of BIP Effectiveness: Refusal frequency and task initiation data will be graphed monthly and reviewed by the IEP team. The team will compare data to baseline to evaluate progress toward IEP Goal #4 (behavioral compliance/task initiation). If no measurable reduction in refusals is observed within 6 weeks of consistent implementation, the team will convene to review the BIP and consider adjustments to task demand level, reward menu, or First-Then structure. (4) Parent Communication: Parents will receive a copy of the BIP and will be informed of Marcus’s progress through quarterly written reports. Staff will contact the family if refusal behaviors increase in frequency or intensity from baseline. Parent input on Marcus’s preferred motivators and home routines will be solicited quarterly to maintain alignment between home and school behavioral supports.
