Pattern of Behavior (describe the pattern of behavior(s) that impedes the student’s learning or the learning of others):
Liam demonstrates a persistent pattern of perseverative behavior and emotional outbursts that impede his learning and the learning of classmates. Specifically: (1) Perseverative behaviors (repeating the same question or statement, inability to disengage from a topic or task even when redirected) occurring 3–5 times per day during high-demand or high-fatigue periods; (2) Verbal emotional outbursts (verbal refusal, crying, statements such as “I can’t do this” or “I used to be good at this”) occurring 2–3 times per day, predominantly in afternoon sessions; (3) Task refusal on cognitively demanding assignments occurring 1–2 times per day; (4) Occasional low-level physical expressions of frustration (slamming books, pushing materials off desk) occurring 1–2 times per week. These behaviors occur most frequently during multi-step tasks, timed assignments, high-noise settings, and afternoon instructional periods when fatigue is greatest. Function is assessed as escape from cognitively overwhelming tasks and frustration from perceived loss of ability.
Instructional and Environmental Conditions or Circumstances (describe the instructional and environmental conditions or circumstances that trigger/contribute to the pattern of behavior(s) being addressed):
Perseverative behaviors and emotional outbursts are most frequent under the following conditions: (1) During multi-step tasks requiring working memory across extended time without checkpoints (e.g., long writing assignments, multi-step math problems); (2) During afternoon instructional periods (after 12:30 PM) when fatigue is greatest; (3) During timed tasks or assessments where speed is a component; (4) In high-noise or high-stimulation environments (e.g., open-plan classroom, assemblies, busy transitions). Antecedent FBA data indicate that approximately 70% of behavioral incidents occur after 12:00 PM and are preceded by a high-demand academic task. Incidents are rare during morning sessions, small-group pull-out, or low-demand independent work. Function of behaviors is assessed as escape from cognitively overwhelming tasks and frustration-based response to perceived inability.
Alternative Behaviors and Skills to be Taught and Monitored (describe the alternative behaviors and skills to be taught and monitored in order to address the pattern of behaviors and the instructional and environmental conditions/ circumstances previously described):
The following alternative behaviors and skills will be explicitly taught and monitored: (1) Cognitive Load Signal: Liam will be directly taught and rehearsed to use a pre-agreed signal (placing a yellow card on his desk) to communicate cognitive overload or fatigue to staff before frustration escalates to a behavioral incident. Staff will acknowledge the signal within 30 seconds and provide a brief break, task modification, or scaffolded support. (2) Break Request Procedure: When Liam places his signal card, he will be offered a 3–5 minute brain break (quiet seated rest, controlled breathing, or low-demand fidget activity) in a low-stimulation corner of the classroom. He will be taught to return to the task independently following the break. (3) Task Decomposition Strategy: Liam will be taught to use a “chunk and check” approach for multi-step tasks: breaking the task into numbered steps on a card and checking off each step as completed. This will be rehearsed during pull-out sessions and prompted by staff during independent work. (4) Cognitive Self-Monitoring: Liam will use a brief end-of-task self-reflection card (rating: “Was this hard? What helped me?”) following cognitively demanding tasks to build metacognitive awareness and support strategy generalization. (5) Fatigue Communication Skill: Liam will be taught to use a verbal phrase (“I need to slow down”) or written signal to communicate fatigue to staff, enabling proactive schedule modifications (e.g., switching to lower-demand activity in the afternoon) rather than reactive escalation management.
Reinforcers of Desired Behaviors (describe how the alternative behaviors will be reinforced):
Desired behaviors will be reinforced using the following strategies, selected based on Liam’s identified motivators (adult recognition, soccer/sports activities, peer status, and progress visualization): (1) Behavior-Specific Praise: Staff will provide immediate, specific verbal praise when Liam uses his signal card, completes a brain break and returns to task, or successfully completes a multi-step task using his chunk-and-check card (e.g., “Liam, I saw you use your signal and then come right back — that takes real self-awareness”). Praise will emphasize strategy use and effort rather than output volume. (2) Visual Progress Tracker: Liam will maintain a weekly progress chart showing days with 1 or fewer behavioral incidents. When he achieves 3 consecutive successful days, he earns a preferred activity (e.g., 10 minutes of soccer skill videos, leading a class game, or choosing his work partner for the next project). (3) Mastery Framing: Staff will regularly highlight examples of Liam’s current academic progress relative to his own earlier post-injury baseline (not pre-injury comparisons), framing growth in terms he can visually track. This addresses the frustration and demoralization that frequently precede outbursts. (4) Reduced Cognitive Load as Immediate Reward: Proactive task modification (e.g., reducing number of problems, allowing oral response) following a successful break request functions as both an accommodation and a positive contingency, reinforcing the break request behavior over escalation.
Methods to Ensure Consistency of Implementation (describe the method(s) that will be used to ensure the consistency of the implementation of the positive behavioral interventions across the student’s school-sponsored instruction or activities (e.g., data collection, frequency, assessment of effectiveness of BIP, additional training for staff, etc.)):
The following methods will be used to ensure consistent implementation of the BIP across all school-sponsored instruction and activities: (1) Data Collection: The special education teacher or paraeducator will collect daily frequency data on behavioral incidents (perseveration, verbal outburst, task refusal) and successful cognitive load signal uses using a structured tally sheet. Time-of-day distribution of incidents will be noted to track fatigue patterns. Data will be reviewed weekly by the special education teacher and graphed monthly for team review. (2) Staff Communication and Training: All instructional staff who work with Liam (general education teacher, special education teacher, paraeducator, PE and specials teachers) will receive a copy of the BIP and attend a 30-minute orientation prior to implementation. Training will include recognition of early fatigue and cognitive overload signs, appropriate response to the signal card, and how to implement proactive task modifications. The BIP will be reviewed monthly during IEP team check-ins to assess fidelity. (3) Assessment of BIP Effectiveness: Incident frequency and signal card use data will be graphed monthly and reviewed by the IEP team. The team will compare data to baseline to evaluate progress toward IEP Goal #4 (behavioral regulation) and Goal #2 (executive function strategy use). If no measurable reduction in incidents is observed within 6 weeks of consistent implementation, the team will convene to review the BIP and consider adjustments to the fatigue schedule or task modification protocol. (4) Parent Communication: Parents will receive a copy of the BIP and will be informed of Liam’s progress through quarterly written reports. Staff will contact the family if behavioral incidents increase in frequency or intensity from baseline, or if new neurological symptoms are observed. A shared fatigue log between home and school will be maintained on a weekly basis to coordinate supports.
